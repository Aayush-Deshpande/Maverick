# The Engineering Story

Project ANUMAAN was built for SIH Problem Statement 26054, DRDO's brief for an AI-enabled real-time digital twin system for health monitoring, fault prediction, and mission reliability enhancement of aero piston engines used in MALE UAVs. The problem statement asked for six things at once: a digital twin core, a health monitoring layer, fault detection and predictive analytics, an AI/ML layer, simulation and replay, and a visualization dashboard. This chapter traces how the team, Midnight Ciphers, got from that six-part brief to the system described in the rest of this documentation: the sequence of decisions, and in two important cases the corrections to earlier decisions, that shaped the engineering.

## Research before implementation

Before any production code was written against the problem statement, the team built a first-principles study course covering the entire domain: engine sensors and fault signatures, CAN bus and ECU acquisition, telemetry constraints, the case for splitting computation between edge and ground, vibration analysis from sampling theory through order tracking and envelope demodulation, anomaly detection, fault diagnosis, remaining useful life estimation, digital twin theory, mission simulation, datasets, system architecture, and evaluation methodology. Twenty-three parts, each starting from first principles and building forward into a design decision for ANUMAAN specifically, rather than treating the domain as already familiar.

This ordering mattered. A team that starts writing anomaly detection code before it has worked through what a residual actually means, or before it understands why fixed-frequency vibration analysis fails on an engine whose speed is constantly changing, ends up with a system that looks complete but cannot answer questions about why it works. The study course is the record of the team doing that groundwork first, and later chapters of this documentation site draw directly on the reasoning it produced.

## Building the physics layer

The most consequential decision to come out of that research phase was to make fault detection depend on physics, not on prescribed synthetic vibration. A simpler approach would have been to author vibration signatures directly: write code that says "when this fault is active, output this waveform." That is fast to build, but every downstream signature has to be authored by hand, and a fault nobody anticipated produces no signature at all.

ANUMAAN's physics layer instead computes cylinder pressure from slider-crank kinematics and a Wiebe heat-release function, propagates that through gas and inertial torque to produce crank angular velocity as a function of crank angle, and derives everything else from that. A misfire in this model is a cylinder genuinely producing no heat release for one combustion cycle. The angular velocity dip, the growth in half-order vibration energy, and the exhaust gas temperature drop that follow are not separately authored; they emerge from the same physics that produces normal engine behavior. Per-cylinder torque deficit at each cylinder's firing angle becomes the basis for per-cylinder misfire detection, and cycle-to-cycle variation in that quantity becomes a proxy for combustion instability.

The vibration analysis built on top of this uses tach-synchronous order tracking rather than fixed-frequency spectral analysis, because a UAV engine's shaft speed changes continuously with throttle and a fixed-frequency bin loses resolution as speed changes. Angular resampling converts the time-domain vibration signal into the angle domain so a mechanical event, a gear mesh, a bearing defect, a cylinder firing, lands at the same order regardless of engine speed. Hilbert envelope demodulation then reveals amplitude-modulated bearing and gear defect signatures that a raw spectrum would hide. Reference engine constants, bore, stroke, compression ratio, firing order, for the Rotax 912 iS and 914 come from the manufacturer's own maintenance manual, so the physics model is grounded in published specification rather than invented numbers. A residual detector, novelty coder, or Bayesian diagnostic network can only be as honest as the physics feeding it, which is why this layer is treated elsewhere in this documentation as the foundation the rest of the system stands on.

## The competitive audit and its self-corrections

Partway through the project, the team conducted a competitive audit: a direct read of every public implementation of PS-26054 it could find, scored against a stated rubric, with ANUMAAN scored on the same rubric as every competitor. The first version placed ANUMAAN third, based on a review of the team's own documentation rather than its code. Reading the actual code changed that assessment, in the team's favor on two of three points: a claimed aliasing error in the spectral analyser turned out not to exist, since the module explicitly checks against the Nyquist frequency and falls back to an envelope path, and a claimed absence of anti-leakage safeguards turned out to be wrong, since the evaluation records showed strict mission-level group isolation across training, validation, and test splits. A third finding, that evaluation rigor was the team's weakest axis, was revised upward once the audit examined the actual numbers: held-out test-mission accuracy reported separately from validation accuracy, the gap between the two disclosed rather than hidden, and a full confusion matrix. That combination moved the team's score from 74 to 79 and its rank from third to second.

A second sweep of the competitive field, conducted afterward, produced two further corrections that narrowed claims the team had made about its own position, rather than strengthening them. The first audit had claimed no team in the field did real vibration signal processing; the second sweep found two teams doing FFT-based vibration analysis at kilohertz rates, and the claim was narrowed to what actually held: no team, including those two, was doing tach-synchronous order tracking or crank-angle-resolved per-cylinder misfire detection, which remained specific to ANUMAAN. More consequential, the team's own dataset documentation had stated that no public dataset existed combining real aero piston engine telemetry with the UAV platform class in the problem statement. That was wrong: NASA's ACES program recorded real operational flight telemetry from the Altus II UAV, powered by a four-cylinder Rotax 914 Turbo, the exact engine class the problem statement targets. The team corrected its own dataset documentation rather than leaving the overclaim in place.

The direction of these corrections is what makes them evidence rather than incidental process detail. Two of the three first-audit corrections improved the team's position, an outcome any team would be tempted to simply accept without scrutiny. The second-sweep corrections narrowed the team's own claims instead, the harder kind to make voluntarily. Both directions appear in the same audit trail, each documented with its reasoning in a form a technical evaluator can check against the actual code and data.

## The novelty-coding honesty correction

A parallel correction happened in the research notes for Bio-Inspired Sparse Novelty Coding, the expand-and-sparsify method modeled on the fruit fly olfactory circuit that ANUMAAN uses for onboard novelty detection. An early description of this component called it "not yet published for engine prognostics and health management as far as I could find," implying the approach itself was original. A proper literature search showed that framing was wrong, and the project's own documentation says so directly. Sparse coding and dictionary learning for machinery fault diagnosis is an established research area with roughly a decade of published work, including online adaptive dictionary learning for bearing faults and shift-invariant sparse coding for rotating machinery. Hyperdimensional computing, the same expand-and-sparsify family under a different name, is already deployed for industrial anomaly detection at the edge. Every property the team might have claimed as its own, sparsity, explainability through active units, online adaptation, was already published in this same application domain.

The corrected claim is narrower and more defensible: not a new algorithm, but a new combination. Applying an established sparse-coding and hyperdimensional-computing-family technique to aero piston engine vibration and physics residuals together, under the bandwidth constraint of a UAV downlink that cannot carry raw high-rate vibration data, is where the team's contribution actually sits. The research notes instruct that this honest phrasing be used going forward: an established family applied to a combination of machine class, deployment constraint, and fault set the team did not find published elsewhere, never a claim that the underlying technique itself was invented. This is the same pattern as the competitive audit correction: an early claim reached for more originality than the evidence supported, a deeper literature pass caught it, and the documentation records the retraction alongside the narrower claim that replaced it.

## The mission planning evolution

The mission planning and reliability system shows the same discipline applied to implementation, visible as a documented before-and-after rather than a single retraction. An architecture blueprint, written before implementation began, opened with an honest audit of what existed: engine physics modeling, residual detection, Bayesian diagnosis, and 3D visualization were operational across all five engine platforms, but the mission planning layer that was supposed to tie propulsion health to mission-level decisions did not exist as an integrated capability. The blueprint listed, subsystem by subsystem, what was fully built, partially built, and not yet started, and set the governing constraint for what came next: the mission loop had to run entirely inside the web-based ground control station, and every displayed number had to come from an actual mathematical model.

The final implementation report, written after the work was done, shows the same subsystem list filled in. A single authoritative mission executive now drives UAV kinematics, ISA atmospheric conditions, and the selected engine's physics runtime on every simulation tick. Faults injected through a pre-flight schedule or live during the simulation propagate through the same physics twin used elsewhere in the system, produce genuine sensor residuals, trigger the novelty detector, activate diagnostic classification, and recalculate mission completion reliability in real time. A completed or aborted mission is automatically packaged into persistent telemetry and manifest files and indexed for replay, closing the loop the blueprint had identified as missing. Backend test suites and a browser-based verification pass covering the full mission simulation workflow both passed against the finished implementation.

Read together, the two documents are a real before-and-after pair on the same subsystem: an honest accounting of a gap, followed by a report against that same accounting once the gap was closed. That is stronger evidence than either document alone, because the blueprint was written without knowledge of how the implementation would turn out, and can be checked directly against its own criteria.

## What the process demonstrated

Two threads run through this chronicle. The first is physics-first design: build the equations that produce normal engine behavior before building the code that detects departures from it, because a detector layered on top of authored signals can only be as honest as the person who wrote them, while a detector layered on top of real thermodynamics inherits whatever the physics actually does, including fault modes nobody thought to author by hand.

The second is a discipline of checking claims before presenting them, consistent enough to leave a visible trail: a competitive score corrected upward after reading actual code instead of trusting a summary, a competitive claim narrowed after a second research sweep found competitors the first pass missed, a dataset claim corrected after search turned up a real public source the team had said did not exist, and a novelty claim retracted and replaced with a narrower one after a literature search showed the original framing was wrong. None of these corrections were forced by an outside reviewer. Each one shows up because the team went looking for the ways its own claims might be wrong, and wrote down what it found.
