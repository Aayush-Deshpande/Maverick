ANUMAAN: AI-Enabled Real-Time Digital Twin for MALE UAV Aero-Piston Engines
DRDO Smart India Hackathon Problem Statement 26054
Team Midnight Ciphers
1. The problem, in one paragraph
Medium Altitude Long Endurance unmanned aerial vehicles fly long intelligence, surveillance and reconnaissance missions, communication relay and maritime surveillance sorties, where the engine cannot afford to surprise anyone. Right now engine health monitoring on these platforms is basically a set of thresholds. A sensor crosses a line, a warning light comes on, and by then the fault has already happened, which is reactive, not predictive. Nobody is estimating what is going on inside the engine that sensors cannot see directly, nobody is catching the degradation trend early enough to predict a failure before it occurs, nobody is giving a trustworthy number for remaining useful life, and there is no way to simulate how an engine would behave on a given mission profile before actually flying it. A piston engine failure mid-sortie means an aborted mission, a lost airframe, or a bad forced landing. What DRDO is asking for is one connected, scalable, modular digital twin framework that does real-time state estimation, health monitoring, anomaly detection, fault prediction, remaining useful life estimation and mission replay together, not five disconnected dashboards.
2. What we built
ANUMAAN means inference in Sanskrit and Hindi, which is roughly what the system does all day. It is a digital twin, but we want to be specific about what that word means here, because it gets thrown around loosely at every hackathon. A twin that just spins a three-dimensional engine model on screen while numbers scroll next to it has not done anything useful, it is a dashboard wearing a costume. Our twin runs an actual state observer, a continuous-discrete Extended Kalman Filter, tracking the engine alongside a physics-informed zero-dimensional and one-dimensional thermodynamic model of that same engine, so it can estimate things no sensor on the aircraft actually measures. Peak combustion pressure inside the cylinder. Turbine inlet temperature before the exhaust cools. The thickness of the oil film inside a bearing, measured in microns, the number that tells you a bearing is about to seize long before anyone would notice from outside. None of these have a physical sensor. All of them come out of the twin, continuously, synchronized to live engine sensor data.
We modelled five engines, not one, because we did not want to build something that only works on a single convenient demo unit. The Rotax 912 iS, naturally aspirated, the Rotax 914 F Turbo and 915 iS Turbo, both turbocharged, the Austro AE300 diesel running heavy aviation fuel, and the VRDE Jayem 2.2 litre, DRDO's own indigenous heavy-fuel engine built for the Tapas-BH-201 platform. Including the VRDE engine mattered to us specifically. This problem statement is rooted in self-reliance for unmanned aerial vehicle propulsion, and an architecture that only works on imported Rotax engines would be missing that point entirely. It also meant the system genuinely had to generalize across naturally aspirated, turbocharged and compression-ignition combustion, not get quietly tuned until one engine looked good on demo day.
3. Why physics first, then AI on top
Here is the honest reason we did not just throw raw engine sensor data at a neural network and call it done. A pure data-driven model is only as good as the flight conditions it was trained on, and the moment the engine sees something outside that envelope, an altitude it has not seen, a throttle transient it was never shown, the model's confidence means nothing. That is basically why nobody certifies black-box artificial intelligence for safety-critical decisions. A pure physics model with no learning, on the other hand, is blind to slow wear, to sensor drift that creeps in over fifty flight hours, to the kind of subtle pattern a human maintenance crew would only catch on a hunch. So we use physics as the ground truth for what normal looks like, and the artificial intelligence and machine learning layer only looks at the gap between what physics predicted and what the sensor actually said. This is the hybrid thermodynamic and data-driven approach the problem statement lists as a desired innovation area, and it is the only approach that made sense once we thought about what an airworthiness reviewer would actually ask.
Before that gap gets computed, every sensor reading passes a range check, a rate-of-change check, and a cross-check against the other sensors through analytical redundancy, essentially sensor fusion applied to fault detection, because a broken sensor reading is not engine degradation, it is a broken sensor, and feeding that straight into a machine learning model raises exactly the kind of false alarm that scrubs a mission for nothing. This check isolates a bad sensor within about 40 milliseconds and lets the system fall back to an estimated value instead of panicking the whole pipeline.
Once a reading clears that check, we track seven channels as a gap from expected, not a raw number: exhaust gas temperature, cylinder head temperature, oil pressure, oil temperature, manifold pressure, fuel flow, and the temperature spread across cylinders, which is how we watch for combustion instability before it becomes a real problem. Because the physics model already knows what these should read at the current altitude, ambient temperature and throttle position, these seven channels stay flat noise the entire time the engine is healthy. A climb does not move them. A cold morning does not move them. Only actual degradation moves them, and even then we do not trust one channel alone, a torque deficit on one cylinder has to show up as a matching exhaust temperature drop on that same cylinder before we call it a confirmed fault instead of a twitchy sensor.
4. The physics core, kept short on purpose
Underneath all of this sits a real thermodynamic model of the engine, airflow, combustion, crankshaft motion, lubrication, running continuously alongside live telemetry. We are not walking through the equations here, this document is meant to be readable in one sitting, but the important design decision is that every fault we simulate is a real physical event, not a switch someone flipped in a script. A misfire in our simulation is an actual loss of combustion on one cylinder for that power stroke, and the drop in engine speed, the exhaust temperature collapse, the vibration spike, all come out of that one physical cause together, the same way they would in a real engine. An overheating trend shows up the same honest way, rising cylinder head temperature feeding the same thermodynamic model rather than a scripted ramp. That is also what makes the AI layer trustworthy instead of superstitious, it never has to guess what normal means for the current flight condition, physics already told it.
5. What the AI actually does
Physics hands over a residual signal. Everything after that is AI, running in four stages that hand off to each other: first, is anything unusual happening at all, the anomaly detection layer. Second, if yes, which component is responsible, the fault diagnosis layer. Third, how much life does that component have left, the prognostics layer producing a remaining useful life estimate. Fourth, what should the operator do about it right now, the decision-support layer. We deliberately built every stage to be explainable rather than one black-box model spitting out a number, because explainable artificial intelligence is not a nice-to-have here, it is the difference between a diagnosis a maintenance engineer can act on and one they have to just trust blindly.
6. The fly brain layer, the part we are most excited about
Before the system tries to diagnose anything, it has to answer a cheaper question constantly, every tick, on modest edge computing hardware: does anything look off. This has to run continuously without eating the compute or datalink bandwidth budget, so we borrowed a trick from an actual fruit fly. A fruit fly smells through about fifty receptor types. Its brain expands those fifty signals onto a much larger population of neurons, each neuron listening to only a small random handful of the receptors, and a winner-take-all step shuts off everything except the loudest few percent. What is left is a sparse fingerprint, similar smells produce similar fingerprints, different smells barely overlap. This got formalized in machine learning as FlyHash, a form of locality-sensitive hashing that needs zero training, the random wiring is fixed once by a seed and left alone.
We run our vibration and physics residual signal through the same trick. Expand it into a bigger sparse space with a fixed random projection, keep only the loudest slice, compare the fingerprint against a library built up during normal flight, a form of lightweight onboard analytics rather than a heavy model running continuously. Close match, nothing to see. Almost no overlap with anything in the library, that is novelty, and that wakes up the heavier diagnostic stage. Because the projection never trains, this runs in flat, predictable time, cheap enough for a genuinely constrained embedded system, and the library builds itself from ordinary flying, needing zero labelled fault examples to start working, which matters given how little real fault data exists for these engines anywhere. What we like about it is that it catches a joint shift across several channels at once, a slow rise in head temperature on a couple of cylinders together with a tiny vibration shift, none of which individually crosses any threshold, but together is exactly what early cooling degradation looks like. A threshold-based system, the kind the problem statement itself calls out as reactive, would sit there quietly until it got much worse.
7. Once something looks off: diagnosis
When novelty fires, a second model, an autoencoder trained only on what normal looks like, tries to reconstruct the current pattern and measures how badly it fails. We check that error against a threshold set by Extreme Value Theory, fitting a Generalized Pareto Distribution to the tail of the error instead of somebody picking a number by hand, which holds our false alarm rate around one in ten thousand, roughly one false alarm every three flight hours, without constantly retuning it. A confirmed anomaly also has to persist for at least three seconds, killing electrical noise spikes, and it has to survive the sensor cross-check first, so a flaky probe does not get treated as an engine emergency.
Fault isolation runs on a Bayesian belief network reasoning over ten failure modes drawn from a Failure Mode, Effects and Criticality Analysis taxonomy built to Military Standard 1629A, covering ignition, combustion, fuel injector and delivery faults, cooling, lubrication, sensor faults and mechanical wear, each with its own residual signature and severity grade. A single-cylinder misfire looks nothing like a bearing going bad. The misfire shows up fast, exhaust gas temperature drop, vibration spike, engine speed dip, all within seconds, graded the most severe category with a detection window under thirty seconds. Bearing wear plays out over hours, and is graded and escalated on a much longer clock. A coolant system fault shows cylinder head temperature creeping across multiple cylinders together rather than one alone. A sensor that is simply drifting shows one channel wandering off while everything that should corroborate it stays flat, correctly graded a minor sensor nuisance rather than an engine emergency, which is the whole point of the cross-check. Every diagnosis comes with a Shapley Additive Explanations breakdown of which channels drove the call and by how much, so a propulsion engineer gets a reason, not just a label, our actual answer to the explainable artificial intelligence requirement. Once confirmed, a fault resolves into a fixed maintenance advisory, root cause, corrective action, emergency checklist, built from deterministic rules rather than anything generative, because what reaches a pilot mid-mission needs to be the same answer every time.
We also check our own homework here. The system can run against a second, independently built engine simulation with its own sensor bias and noise, kept separate from the model used for detection, so we are testing against something we did not build the detector to already agree with.
8. How much life is left
Real aero engines are never run until they break, they get overhauled at conservative time-between-overhaul limits, and there is basically no public run-to-failure data for aero-piston engines the way there is for the turbofan datasets most academic prognostics papers train on. A confident single number like fourteen point two hours remaining is not impressive, it is reckless. A mission commander reads that, launches a thirteen hour sortie, and if the real number was eleven hours the airframe is gone. So we never give a single number. We run two independent remaining useful life estimates side by side, one from physical damage accumulation and one from how the residual channels are drifting over time, and when the two disagree by more than expected, we surface that disagreement to the propulsion engineer rather than averaging it away.
The combined estimate gets wrapped in a statistically calibrated range using Split Conformal Prediction, calibrated on whole past missions rather than individual moments, because moments inside one flight are correlated in a way separate missions are not. That gives a real mathematical guarantee: the true remaining life falls inside the stated range at least ninety five percent of the time, regardless of what the underlying error distribution actually looks like. On screen this shows up as a range, something like 142 to 186 hours at ninety five percent confidence, and mission planning always checks against the conservative low end, never the middle number, because the low end is what keeps a launch decision honest.
9. Will this engine finish this mission
Knowing an engine is healthy right now does not answer the question an operator is actually asking before takeoff. ANUMAAN computes an actual mission reliability number, the probability the mission finishes without an abort, given current engine health, the planned flight profile and current environmental conditions, by running the mission forward many times with randomized failure draws across each flight phase from taxi to landing. The same logic drives live glide reachability: given current altitude, speed and the aircraft's glide characteristics, it draws a three-dimensional footprint of ground the aircraft could reach with the engine dead, ranks nearby airfields inside that footprint, and keeps updating as the aircraft moves. It also supports mission replay of historical flight data for post-flight analysis, and can simulate a planned mission under high-altitude, hot-weather, or rapid-throttle-transition conditions before anyone actually flies it, matching the simulation and replay capability the problem statement asks for.
10. How it is wired together
The architecture follows the standard six-layer condition monitoring model this field uses: data acquisition, data manipulation, state detection, health assessment, prognostics, advisory generation, each layer feeding the next. Onboard, telemetry reaches an edge computing node over a Controller Area Network bus, using the Linux SocketCAN interface, the kind of interface that would sit behind an Engine Control Unit or a Full Authority Digital Engine Control on a real aircraft, where the sensor cross-check runs and a compressed spectral summary of raw vibration gets pulled out, because one vibration sensor produces more data per second than a tactical unmanned aerial vehicle's downlink can carry alongside flight control and video. Only that compressed summary and about thirty scalar engine channels, sampled twenty times a second, cross the air-to-ground link. On the ground, or in a cloud or local server deployment depending on what the mission needs, the physics twin, the Kalman filter, both AI stages, the mission reliability engine and the three-dimensional view run inside the ground control station, where compute is not the constraint it is in the air. We budgeted the whole chain, sensor to screen, at under two hundred milliseconds, so the human machine interface does not visibly lag the real aircraft during a high-stress maneuver.
The ground station runs two modes off one backend, because we built it modular rather than locking everything to one engine. A fleet mode running all five engines at once for side-by-side comparison, and a deep mode for the Rotax 912 iS specifically, carrying the full diagnosis, prognostics and replay stack in the most detail, since that is our primary reference platform. The interface is split by who is looking at it, a pilot screen showing power margin and reachability with a one-click divert, and a separate propulsion engineer console showing residuals, estimated internal states and the three-dimensional thermal view, because a pilot mid-maneuver and an engineer on the ground need the same underlying truth shown two different ways.
11. The three-dimensional twin
Each of the five engines has a three-dimensional model that runs in the browser at full frame rate on an ordinary workstation or tablet, no native application required. It is not a separate animation running next to the real data, it is driven by the same telemetry and fault state as everything else, so when the diagnostic layer isolates a fault to a specific part, that part lights up directly on the model. That gives a maintenance engineer something to actually look at instead of a row number in a spreadsheet. We also built a higher-fidelity version and a simulated flight environment for deeper visual validation outside the browser client.
12. Learning across a fleet without leaking anything
An engine at a freezing high-altitude airstrip and an engine in desert heat wear out differently, and one model trained centrally on both will fit neither particularly well. We use federated learning to handle this, one of the desired innovation areas the problem statement names directly, but only at the post-flight ground depot, never over the tactical radio link mid-mission, and raw flight data never gets centralized. The physics model itself never changes. Only the learned anomaly detector gets updated, through a small, lightweight parameter-efficient update rather than retraining the whole thing, so no single base's update can do much damage. Each base trains locally with a regularization term that keeps its update anchored to the shared model, so one base's unusual conditions cannot drag the global model off course. Before any update leaves a depot it gets clipped and has calibrated statistical noise added, giving a formal, provable differential privacy guarantee, meaning patrol patterns and mission tempo cannot be reconstructed from anything that crosses the network, while the shared model still specializes to each base's real conditions. This is what secure telemetry architecture should mean, not just encryption on the wire but a guarantee about what can be inferred from what gets transmitted at all. Fleet-wide flight hours also feed survival statistics tied to environmental exposure, so a base running harsher heat cycles automatically gets its predictive maintenance schedule pulled forward, the autonomous maintenance advisory piece working as intended.
13. Why we think this one is different
A lot of teams will show up with a spinning CAD model hooked to a random number generator and call it a digital twin. Ours estimates internal engine states that sensors cannot see, from real physics, not fake telemetry dressed up to look convincing. Every fault call comes with a reason, not a confidence score, which matters because nothing uninterpretable gets certified on an aircraft. Remaining life is a calibrated range with an actual mathematical guarantee behind it, not an invented number that sounds precise. Health status turns into a specific instruction, derate, divert, descend, not a chart somebody has to interpret under pressure. It runs on five real engines including an indigenous one, not one convenient demo unit. It respects what can and cannot cross a tactical downlink instead of pretending bandwidth is free. And fleet learning stays at the depot with real privacy guarantees instead of being a buzzword bolted on.
14. What we are delivering
A working prototype covering the full chain, simulated or replayed telemetry through physics-based state estimation, artificial intelligence fault detection, remaining useful life estimation and mission reliability, running end to end rather than stitched together for a demo video. A digital twin architecture across five engine platforms. A ground control station human machine interface with separate pilot and engineer views and a synchronized three-dimensional visualization. An artificial intelligence and machine learning layer doing anomaly detection, fault isolation and calibrated prognostics. Full technical documentation covering the physics model, the AI layer, the system architecture and a realistic deployment roadmap toward defence-grade certification, because we would rather be upfront about that distance than pretend a hackathon prototype is flight-ready. It is not, yet. But the architecture was built so that getting there is engineering and certification work, not a redesign.
